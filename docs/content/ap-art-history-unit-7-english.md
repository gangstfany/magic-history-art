## AP #181 · Petra, Jordan: Treasury and Great Temple

### Identification

Petra, Jordan; Nabataean, with Hellenistic/Ptolemaic and Roman connections; c. 400 B.C.E.–100 C.E. in the AP entry. Makers are unrecorded. Cut rock characterizes the Treasury; the Great Temple also uses constructed sandstone masonry.

### Function

The Treasury probably served an elite or royal funerary purpose, not treasury storage. The so-called Great Temple's precise use is debated; seating supports civic assembly or audience use during part of its history.

### Content

The entry combines the city landscape, the Treasury façade, and the separate Great Temple complex. Streets, public spaces, and water systems show an inhabited metropolis, not merely a cemetery.

### Form

Carvers removed sandstone to produce the Treasury's two-story architectural façade. Corinthian columns, a broken pediment, and a central tholos adapt Hellenistic vocabulary. The Great Temple's terraces and freestanding colonnades organize open urban space.

### Context

Nabataean caravan commerce supported monumental building and cultural exchange. The AP date span applies to the collective entry, not a secure date for every component. Neither classical ornament nor trade proves that outsiders built the city.

### Recognition Anchors

Look for rose sandstone, the upper broken pediment framing a round tholos, and the Great Temple's open terraces. Do not confuse the Treasury with Petra's Monastery.

### Comparisons

AP #182, Bamiyan Buddha: both transform a cliff through subtractive carving, but a funerary architectural façade differs from a Buddhist body. AP #26, Athenian Agora: both belong to urban public life; the Great Temple's exact institutional use remains less certain.

### Required Views and Sources

## AP #182 · Buddha

### Identification

Bamiyan, Afghanistan; Gandharan Buddhist traditions; c. 400–800 C.E. in the AP label. Cut rock with plaster and polychrome paint; makers and patrons are unrecorded. Both monumental standing Buddhas were destroyed in 2001.

### Function

The images made Buddhist presence visible across the valley and offered devotional focuses to local communities and travelers. Passages associated with the figures enabled circumambulation, linking bodily movement with worship.

### Content

Two giant Buddhas occupied tall cliff niches amid caves and monastic spaces. The principal AP photograph emphasizes the western figure. Proposed identities such as Vairocana are scholarly interpretations, not uniformly established facts.

### Form

Rock supplied the monumental bodies; plaster and paint completed drapery and surfaces. Parts near the head and feet allowed circulation. Weathered pre-destruction photographs do not preserve the full original appearance.

### Context

The valley connected South and Central Asia along Silk Route branches. Scholars commonly place the colossal images in the sixth–seventh centuries within the broader AP range. The Taliban's March 2001 destruction must be identified as a specific historical act, not a timeless practice of all Muslims.

### Recognition Anchors

Recognize the extraordinarily tall standing figure, cliff niche, and neighboring caves. Distinguish archival photographs of the intact sculpture from present empty niches.

### Comparisons

AP #181, Petra: both use cliff carving, but their forms and functions differ. AP #184, Jowo Rinpoche: both support devotion to the Buddha; fixed monumental stone contrasts with a metal seated image repeatedly dressed and restored.

## AP #183 · The Kaaba

### Identification

Mecca, Saudi Arabia; Islamic sanctuary with pre-Islamic origins. The AP label gives rededication by Muhammad in 631–632 C.E. and multiple renovations. Granite masonry is covered by a silk kiswah with calligraphy in gold- and silver-wrapped thread.

### Function

The Kaaba is Islam's holiest sanctuary and the focus of qibla, the direction of prayer. Pilgrims perform tawaf, seven circuits around it. Worship is directed to God, not to the building as a deity.

### Content

The Black Stone is set into a corner. Qur'anic calligraphy ornaments the covering. The Kaaba is the sanctuary within the courtyard of the Masjid al-Haram, not the whole mosque complex.

### Form

A compact, approximately cubical volume contrasts with the vast surrounding congregation. The black covering, metallic calligraphic band, and door curtain give a restrained structure powerful visual distinction.

### Context

Successive rebuildings and textile renewals sustain the site. Islamic tradition associates its foundation with Ibrahim and Ismail; this religious account should not be presented as an archaeologically documented construction date. The broad AP rededication label does not date every event in Muhammad's return and final pilgrimage.

### Recognition Anchors

Identify the black-draped cube, gold-colored Arabic inscriptions, and circular movement of pilgrims. The gathering context reveals how architecture organizes collective ritual.

### Comparisons

AP #185, Dome of the Rock: both are sacred destinations, but only the Kaaba determines global qibla. AP #186, Isfahan mosque: its mihrab marks a prayer direction toward Mecca rather than duplicating the Kaaba's sanctuary function.

### Required Views and Sources

Required IDs: `circumambulation`, `exterior`, `gathering-context`. [College Board, PDF pp. 5–6](https://secure-media.collegeboard.org/digitalServices/pdf/ap/ap-art-history-ced-content-area-7-west-and-central-asia.pdf#page=5); [Smarthistory](https://smarthistory.org/the-kaaba/). Supplied volume four, PDF pp. 199–201. Image selection must preserve the distinction between the sanctuary and its surrounding mosque.

## AP #184 · Jowo Rinpoche, enshrined in the Jokhang Temple

### Identification

Jokhang Temple, Lhasa, Tibet; Yarlung dynastic devotional setting. Traditionally brought to Tibet in 641 C.E.; later restored. Gilt metals, semiprecious stones, pearls, paint, and offerings. The original maker and casting workshop are not securely known.

### Function

Devotees approach the image as the sacred presence of Shakyamuni. Dressing, offerings, and pilgrimage maintain a relationship with the Buddha, rather than treating the statue only as a static art object.

### Content

The historical Buddha sits in padmasana. His left hand meditates; his right calls the earth to witness enlightenment. A rich throne, crown, and robes accumulate devotional additions.

### Form

Gilding gives the seated body a luminous appearance; blue hair and jeweled adornment create strong contrasts. Later repairs and clothing mean its current appearance cannot simply be projected back to 641.

### Context

Tradition links the image's arrival to Princess Wencheng's marriage to Songtsen Gampo. Claims of an exact lifetime portrait of the Buddha express sacred lineage, not verified archaeological history. Lhasa is the historical worship anchor, not proof of local manufacture.

### Recognition Anchors

Look for a gilded seated Buddha, blue hair, an earth-touching hand, and elaborate devotional dress in a shrine setting.

### Comparisons

AP #182, Bamiyan Buddha: both embody Buddhist devotion, but differ in medium, scale, and ritual access. AP #183, Kaaba: both draw pilgrims and undergo renewal; a sacred anthropomorphic presence differs from a nonfigural sanctuary organizing common direction.

### Required Views and Sources

Required ID: `primary`. Examine the image in its devotional setting, not an unrelated gilt Buddha. ## AP #185 · Dome of the Rock

### Identification

Jerusalem; Islamic, Umayyad; completed 691–692 C.E., with renovations. Patron: Caliph Abd al-Malik. Stone masonry and wooden roofing combine with mosaics, later glazed ceramic tile, and renewed gilt metal dome coverings.

### Function

The shrine encloses a sacred rock and communicates religious and political authority. Its original intended purpose remains debated. It should not be reduced to a standard congregational mosque.

### Content

The rock occupies the center. Arabic inscriptions articulate Islamic belief and refer to Mary and Jesus. Vegetal scrolls, jewels, and crown motifs create a rich nonfigural program.

### Form

An octagonal exterior surrounds a central domed space and two ambulatories. Columns, marble, filtered light, and gold-ground mosaics direct attention toward the center and enable movement around it.

### Context

Abd al-Malik built on the Haram al-Sharif/Temple Mount, a site with intersecting sacred histories. The architecture reworks late antique and Byzantine visual traditions. Association with Muhammad's Night Journey belongs to Islamic belief. Exterior tiles and present dome surfaces include later interventions, not only seventh-century fabric.

### Recognition Anchors

Recognize the gold-colored dome above an octagon; inside, identify the exposed rock, surrounding arcades, and nonfigural mosaics and inscriptions.

### Comparisons

AP #51, San Vitale: both use central planning and luminous mosaics, but their imagery articulates different faiths and authorities. AP #183, Kaaba: both support sacred visitation; internal circulation around a rock differs from external tawaf around a cubical sanctuary.

## AP #186 · Great Mosque (Masjid-e Jameh)

### Identification

Isfahan, Iran; Persian Islamic architecture developed under Seljuk, Il-Khanid, Timurid, Safavid, and later patrons. The AP foundation label is c. 700 C.E., with later additions and restorations. Stone, brick, wood, plaster, and glazed ceramic tile.

### Function

The mosque accommodates congregational prayer. Its mihrab marks qibla toward Mecca. Multiple connections to surrounding streets and markets also integrate worship with public mobility and commerce.

### Content

A courtyard, iwans, prayer halls, domes, and a mihrab belong to a layered complex. An iwan is a vaulted hall open on one side; a mihrab is a directional niche, not an altar.

### Form

Four iwans address the central open courtyard. Brick piers and domes articulate prayer spaces. Tile façades and carved plaster inscriptions reveal different materials and building campaigns.

### Context

Eleventh–twelfth-century Seljuk expansion and transformation are crucial: the eleventh-century domed chambers precede the twelfth-century four-iwan courtyard conversion. Later dynasties continued modifying the complex. Do not confuse it with the Safavid Shah/Imam Mosque at Naqsh-e Jahan, or substitute the Met's tile mihrab from a different Isfahan building for the required plaster mihrab.

### Recognition Anchors

Identify monumental courtyard-facing iwans, tile and calligraphic ornament, brick vaults, and the carved plaster mihrab.

### Comparisons

AP #168, Djenné mosque: both join worship with urban community, but brick-and-tile construction differs from earthen architecture and annual replastering. AP #185, Dome of the Rock: both use nonfigural sacred ornament, but courtyard congregational planning differs from a centralized shrine.

## AP #187 · Folio from a Qur'an

### Identification

Arab Islamic, Abbasid; North Africa or Near East, original workshop uncertain. AP date: c. eighth–ninth century C.E. Ink, color, and gold on parchment. Scribes and illuminators are unrecorded.

### Function

The manuscript preserves divine revelation in Arabic and assists recitation. Colored vowel marks, verse divisions, and decorated chapter openings provide navigational cues to readers familiar with the text.

### Content

The identified manuscript opening relates to al-‘Ankabut, the Spider chapter. Sacred words, vegetal ornament, and geometric organization take precedence over narrative figures.

### Form

The broad horizontal page contains Kufic writing with extended horizontal strokes and generous spacing. Arabic reads right to left. Red vowel points and gold chapter decoration distinguish reading aids from the main dark text.

### Context

Costly parchment and controlled proportions honor the text and indicate substantial patron resources. A donation inscription associated with the manuscript establishes its existence by 911 C.E. and a Damascus connection; it does not establish Damascus as its place of manufacture. “Possibly Iraq” is a scholarly suggestion, not a firm localization.

### Recognition Anchors

Look for the landscape-format parchment, angular Kufic, elongated horizontal letters, red points, and gold chapter ornament or marginal palmette.

### Comparisons

AP #189, Bahram Gur: both are luxury manuscript pages, but Qur'anic recitation differs from secular epic illustration. AP #185, Dome of the Rock: both use script and nonfigural decoration to express faith at dramatically different viewing scales.

### Required Views and Sources

## AP #188 · Basin (Baptistère de St. Louis)

### Identification

Muhammad ibn al-Zain; Mamluk Egypt or Syria; c. 1320–1340 C.E. Brass inlaid with gold and silver. The precise workshop and original commissioner remain uncertain.

### Function

The basin was a luxury object for an elite secular setting; its exact original ceremony or use is unresolved. French royal baptism was a later reuse, not evidence of its initial purpose.

### Content

Horsemen, processions, hunting and combat, animals, and roundels cover the vessel. Aquatic creatures appear on its interior floor. The maker signed repeatedly; identifying every figure or heraldic sign remains contentious.

### Form

Incised brass receives precious-metal inlay, producing fine contrasts across a shallow, broad vessel. Continuous bands encourage the viewer to turn around the object. Inner and outer ornament make multiple viewpoints important.

### Context

Mamluk workshops excelled at luxury metalwork. Figural imagery in a secular object refutes a blanket claim that all Islamic art excludes figures. Louis IX died before this basin's production, so its familiar nickname cannot demonstrate his ownership. Paris is a later collection location, not its production anchor.

### Recognition Anchors

Recognize a low, wide brass basin, bright metal inlay, dense figural registers and horseman roundels, rather than a dominant monumental inscription band.

### Comparisons

AP #57, Pyxis of al-Mughira: both elite Islamic objects use figures and animals, but ivory carving differs from metal inlay. AP #189, Bahram Gur: both emphasize equestrian and martial values; the basin's specific identities remain less secure than the epic's named hero.

## AP #189 · Bahram Gur Fights the Karg, folio from the Great Il-Khanid Shahnama

### Identification

Persian Islamic, Il-Khanid; c. 1330–1340 C.E. Ink, opaque watercolor, gold, and silver on paper. An unrecorded painter worked within a court-associated manuscript environment attributed to Tabriz.

### Function

The lavish epic page offers a heroic model of kingship for elite readers. Courage and equestrian skill connect a pre-Islamic Persian king to values prized by Il-Khanid patrons.

### Content

Bahram Gur defeats the Karg while assisting an Indian ruler in the Shahnama narrative. The beast is pictured as a horned wolf; different translations also suggest a rhinoceros. The story's setting is not the painting's manufacture location.

### Form

The mounted hero and fallen creature establish a dramatic focal relationship. Layered rocks and trees evoke Chinese landscape conventions; patterned costume connects to European textile models. Text and painting remain parts of a book page.

### Context

Mongol rule in Iran fostered cosmopolitan court production and appropriation of Persian royal history. Tabriz is a defensible court attribution, but a specific workshop or commission is not securely documented. The dispersed folio should be understood within a larger collaborative manuscript.

### Recognition Anchors

Identify the horse-mounted king, horned beast below, rocky landscape, and epic text adjacent to a colorful miniature.

### Comparisons

AP #190, Court of Gayumars: both illustrate the Shahnama, but martial action differs from hierarchical courtly order. AP #187, Qur'an folio: both display expensive bookmaking, yet secular illustrated epic and sacred calligraphy have different contents and reading practices.

## AP #190 · The Court of Gayumars, folio from Shah Tahmasp's Shahnama

### Identification

Attributed to Sultan Muhammad; Safavid Iran, Tabriz; c. 1522–1525 C.E. in the AP label. Ink, opaque watercolor, and gold on paper. The page belongs to the collaborative royal Shahnama associated with Shah Tahmasp.

### Function

An image of the legendary first Persian king supported elite enjoyment and Safavid royal identity. The manuscript also participated in diplomatic gift culture; it is a secular epic, not a Qur'an.

### Content

Gayumars presides above relatives and leopard-skin-clad subjects in a mountain world shared with animals. The apparently harmonious court carries narrative pathos: his son Siyamak is destined to die in conflict.

### Form

The high central king and curved tiers of attendants create vertical hierarchy. Jewel-like rocks, plants, and minute figures fill shallow, intricately layered space. Image and text boundaries do not behave like a single window of linear perspective.

### Context

Ferdowsi composed the Persian epic around 1000. This royal copy began under Shah Ismail I and developed under Shah Tahmasp, drawing on Tabriz and Herat traditions. Attribution to Sultan Muhammad acknowledges a master painter without denying workshop collaboration.

### Recognition Anchors

Recognize the king seated high on a mountain, a curved gathering in spotted leopard skins, and dense flowers, animals, and colorful rocks.

### Comparisons

AP #189, Bahram Gur: the same epic tradition develops contrasting ideals of heroic action and ordered court. AP #191, Ardabil Carpet: both connect Safavid patronage to virtuoso craft, but figural storytelling differs from an integrated nonfigural textile design.

## AP #191 · The Ardabil Carpet

### Identification

Safavid Iran; 1539–1540 C.E., equivalent to the inscribed AH 946. Silk foundation with knotted wool pile. Maqsud of Kashan is named in the inscription, probably in a supervisory role; individual weavers are unrecorded and the workshop location is uncertain.

### Function

The monumental textile adorned an elite or sacred setting and displayed patronage through skilled labor. It is traditionally associated with the Ardabil shrine of Shaykh Safi al-Din; details of the original commission should retain qualification.

### Content

A central golden medallion anchors floral and vegetal ornament on a blue field. Quarter-medallions occupy corners, two unequal lamps extend from the center, and an end inscription includes Hafez's poetry, Maqsud's name, and the date.

### Form

Dense wool knots on silk structure enable minute curving designs. Border bands unify the huge surface. A proposed perspective explanation for the unequal lamps is a theory, not a demonstrated intention.

### Context

Safavid dynastic identity was linked to the Ardabil Sufi ancestor. Such production required coordinated teams. Neither the carpet's shrine association nor Maqsud's Kashan name proves a weaving city. Later repairs incorporated material from its companion carpet, complicating the present object.

### Recognition Anchors

Identify the gold medallion, dark blue floral field, matching corner quarters, unequal hanging lamps, and inscription cartouche.

### Comparisons

AP #162, All-T'oqapu tunic: both prestigious textiles organize authority through skilled patterning, but a body-worn grid differs from floor-scale radial order. AP #190, Court of Gayumars: both express Safavid luxury through collaborative craft, with different relationships to figures and narrative.
