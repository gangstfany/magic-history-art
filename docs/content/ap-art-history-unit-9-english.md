## AP #213: Nan Madol

### Identification

- Maker/culture: Saudeleur patrons and Pohnpeian builders; individual makers unrecorded; Pohnpeian; Saudeleur dynasty.
- Date: c. 700–1600 C.E.
- Medium: Basalt boulders and prismatic columns.
- Type and period: Megalithic ceremonial and residential complex; Saudeleur political and ceremonial center.
- Source geography: Nan Madol, Pohnpei, Federated States of Micronesia.
- Learning group: Monuments & Ancestors.

Nan Madol's anchor is the actual Pohnpei architectural complex.

### Function

Served as a political, ceremonial and mortuary center for Saudeleur rulers. Its separated islets organized elite residences, ritual activity and burial, while waterways connected them.

### Form

Artificial islets support massive enclosures built with intersecting courses of naturally prismatic basalt. Dry-stone walls and canals shape circulation; the columns are not all individually carved into geometric rods.

### Content

The complex contains palatial, sacred, residential and burial domains. Monumental scale and the movement of heavy stone make chiefly authority visible; no single surviving wall represents the entire city.

### Context

The CED uses a broad c. 700–1600 date. Archaeological accounts place much monumental construction later, especially around 1200–1500; the broad curriculum date is not a date for every wall. Oral histories and archaeological evidence offer different but related ways to understand the site.

### Recognition Anchors

- Intersecting courses of long basalt columns.
- Artificial islets and substantial enclosed walls.
- Waterways immediately adjoining the stone architecture.

### Comparisons

AP #214, Moai on platform (ahu) (`ap214-moai-ahu`): Both mobilize monumental stone for chiefly and ancestral authority; Nan Madol is a canal-linked built complex, whereas moai are anthropomorphic ancestors standing on an ahu.

## AP #214: Moai on platform (ahu)

### Identification

- Maker/culture: Rapa Nui carvers and chiefly communities; individual makers unrecorded; Rapa Nui; Polynesian.
- Date: c. 1100–1600 C.E.
- Medium: Volcanic tuff figures on basalt base.
- Type and period: Ancestral sculpture on ceremonial platform; Rapa Nui ancestral monument traditions.
- Source geography: Rapa Nui (Easter Island), Chile.
- Learning group: Monuments & Ancestors.

The CED identifies Rapa Nui; the required photograph shows moai with red pukao on an ahu. A specific platform is not asserted without separate site verification.

### Function

Moai embody ancestors and sustain relationships between descendants, lineage and chiefly authority. Their ahu platforms serve as ceremonial places rather than neutral museum pedestals.

### Form

Large elongated heads, prominent brows and noses, compact bodies and arms close to the torso stand on a low stone platform. Red pukao on some figures contrast with the darker bodies; not every moai originally had this feature.

### Content

The figures are anthropomorphic ancestral presences, not merely isolated giant heads. Their grouping, orientation and historically installed eyes contribute to ritual engagement and vitality.

### Context

Rapa Nui belongs to networks of Polynesian voyaging. Colonial disruption and subsequent cultural renewal affect how moai are cared for and activated. The required CED photograph does not by itself establish a named platform, so this study does not assign one.

### Recognition Anchors

- Elongated faces with strong brows and projecting noses.
- Figures arranged on a low ahu platform.
- Red pukao on several figures in the required view.

### Comparisons

AP #217, Female deity (`ap217-female-deity`): Both use human form to engage ancestors or divinities; moai operate at monumental outdoor scale, whereas Nukuoro wooden figures participate in temple ritual through compact, highly simplified bodies.

## AP #215: 'Ahu 'ula (feather cape)

### Identification

- Maker/culture: Hawaiian featherworkers and chiefly patrons; individual makers unrecorded; Hawaiian; Polynesian.
- Date: Late 18th century C.E.
- Medium: Feathers and fiber.
- Type and period: Chiefly feather cape; Hawaiian chiefly regalia.
- Source geography: Hawaiian Islands, Polynesia.
- Learning group: Rank & Sacred Regalia.

Hawaiian creation tradition is established; the exact island and featherworking workshop are not fixed by the CED. The British Museum is the present collection, not the production site.

### Function

High-ranking men wore feather regalia for ceremonies and battle. The cape made status, sacred potency and the labor invested in gathering rare feathers visible; its purpose exceeded protection from weather.

### Form

Dense overlapping feather bundles attach to a fiber-net foundation. A curved neckline and semicircular silhouette fit the wearer, while red, yellow and dark areas create crescent-like bands.

### Content

Red was associated with chiefly and divine status, while scarce yellow feathers also carried high value in Hawai'i. Color and material communicate rank together with the identity of the wearer.

### Context

Feather capes circulated as prestigious gifts during encounters with early European visitors. The person who brought this cape to England is unknown. The CED dates the selected work to the late eighteenth century; wider museum dating for related capes should not be mistaken for an exact documented making date.

### Recognition Anchors

- Red and yellow curved feather bands.
- Semicircular cape with a shaped neckline.
- Dense overlapping feathers over a netted foundation.

### Comparisons

AP #216, Staff god (`ap216-staff-god`): Both invest fiber and valued materials with sacred power; the cape displays rank on a chief's body, while the staff god's barkcloth encloses a divine image.

## AP #216: Staff god

### Identification

- Maker/culture: Rarotongan woodcarvers and barkcloth makers; individual makers unrecorded; Rarotongan; Polynesian.
- Date: Late 18th to early 19th century C.E.
- Medium: Wood, tapa, fiber, and feathers.
- Type and period: Wrapped staff-shaped deity image; Rarotongan sacred-image traditions and missionary encounters.
- Source geography: Rarotonga, Cook Islands.
- Learning group: Rank & Sacred Regalia.

The regional carving and ritual context is Rarotonga, not the present London collection; an individual workshop is unknown.

### Function

The wrapped image contained and protected sacred power and could be carried upright on a litter. Its exact deity and full ritual use remain uncertain; identifying it definitively as Tangaroa goes beyond the evidence.

### Form

A long wooden core is enclosed by a thick roll of barkcloth, held by bindings. At the projecting upper end, a large carved head sits above small figures; a feather pendant adds another material and texture.

### Content

The image combines traditionally male woodcarving with female barkcloth production and evokes productive and reproductive capacities. The lower carved end, originally including a phallus, is missing; its present state is not its complete historical form.

### Context

The image was among objects presented to missionary John Williams in 1827, within a broader transformation of religious practices. The required historical engraving is contextual evidence of the encounter setting, not another detail photograph of the object. Its later collecting history does not replace its Rarotongan origin.

### Recognition Anchors

- Very long, thick barkcloth-wrapped staff.
- Projecting carved head and rows of small figures.
- Bindings, layered cloth and attached feathers.

### Comparisons

AP #217, Female deity (`ap217-female-deity`): Both are sacred wooden images. The staff god emphasizes wrapping, attached materials and repeated small figures, while the Female deity uses smooth simplified volumes and could receive separate ritual adornment.

## AP #217: Female deity

### Identification

- Maker/culture: Nukuoro carvers; individual maker unrecorded; Nukuoran; Polynesian outlier in Micronesia.
- Date: c. 18th to 19th century C.E.
- Medium: Wood.
- Type and period: Tino aitu deity figure; Nukuoro temple and harvest-ritual traditions.
- Source geography: Nukuoro Atoll, Federated States of Micronesia.
- Learning group: Monuments & Ancestors.

The Nukuoro ritual and carving tradition is established; the exact carver and original temple are not established for the illustrated figure.

### Function

Tino aitu figures could become resting places for deities or deified ancestors during temple ceremonies associated with harvest cycles. Offerings, dance and adornment made them participants in ritual rather than autonomous abstract ornaments.

### Form

An ovoid head with minimal facial detail, columnar neck, sloping shoulders and long arms organize the body into smooth volumes. Bent legs meet a base. Strong simplification still preserves recognizable human anatomy.

### Content

The selected work represents a female divinity through restrained bodily features. Named figures in Nukuoro traditions were associated with family groups, priests and temples, but those specific affiliations are not securely established for this illustrated figure.

### Context

Nukuoro is geographically Micronesian but culturally and linguistically linked to Polynesia, a Polynesian outlier. Nineteenth-century collecting and Christianization displaced many figures from ritual contexts. Later European admiration of their abstraction should not erase their Nukuoran functions.

### Recognition Anchors

- Ovoid head with almost no facial detail.
- Sloping shoulders and long arms close to the torso.
- Smooth, simplified wooden body and flexed legs.

### Comparisons

AP #214, Moai on platform (ahu) (`ap214-moai-ahu`): Both human-shaped images sustain relations with supernatural or ancestral presences; Nukuoro figures receive adornment and offerings in temple ceremonies, while moai assert ancestral authority on outdoor platforms.

## AP #218: Buk (mask)

### Identification

- Maker/culture: Mabuiag Island mask makers; individual maker unrecorded; Mabuiag Island; Torres Strait Islander.
- Date: Mid- to late 19th century C.E.
- Medium: Turtle shell, wood, fiber, feathers, and shell.
- Type and period: Composite ceremonial mask; Torres Strait turtle-shell masquerade traditions.
- Source geography: Mabuiag Island, Torres Strait, Australia.
- Learning group: Ritual & Performance.

Smarthistory identifies Mabuiag Island for the illustrated Metropolitan Museum mask; this anchors its source community, not a known individual workshop or present New York holding.

### Function

In masquerade traditions, masks worked with costume, music and movement to connect people with ancestral or supernatural presences. For this particular object, use in a local ceremony rather than manufacture for outsiders remains uncertain.

### Form

Stitched turtle-shell components form three stacked registers: a human face, a frigatebird and feathers. Projecting wings, fiber hair and feathers give the assemblage multiple textures and would animate during movement.

### Content

The joined human and bird forms may refer to a hero, ancestor or lineage relationship. Such interpretations are plausible but not secure identifications of a named ancestor or a specific totem.

### Context

The illustrated work is the Metropolitan Museum's specific Mabuiag Island mask. Trade, missionary suppression and collecting shaped the survival of Torres Strait turtle-shell masks; the static museum presentation omits sound, costume and motion.

### Recognition Anchors

- Stacked human-face, bird and feather registers.
- Stitched turtle-shell plates.
- Projecting wings and hanging fiber.

### Comparisons

AP #222, Malagan display and mask (`ap222-malagan-display-mask`): Both link composite materials and masks to performance. Buk joins turtle-shell bird and human forms, while New Ireland malagan masks and carvings participate in specific mortuary and exchange traditions; their roles are not interchangeable.

## AP #219: Hiapo (tapa)

### Identification

- Maker/culture: Niuean barkcloth makers and painters; individual maker unrecorded; Niuean; Polynesian.
- Date: c. 1850–1900 C.E.
- Medium: Tapa or bark cloth, freehand painting.
- Type and period: Painted barkcloth; 19th-century Niuean hiapo traditions.
- Source geography: Niue, Polynesia.
- Learning group: Cloth & Exchange.

Niuean barkcloth tradition is established; the precise maker and workshop are unknown. Auckland is the current collection, not the creation location.

### Function

Barkcloth could serve clothing, display and ceremonial exchange and was valued as women's wealth. The original use of this particular hiapo is not documented, so general textile functions should not become an invented wedding provenance.

### Form

Beaten inner bark forms a nonwoven surface. Fine freehand painting organizes concentric zones, subdivided geometric units and vegetal motifs around the edges; it is not a woven patterned textile.

### Content

Repeated triangles, squares, circular structures and naturalistic vegetal forms produce dense order. Writing and human imagery occur on some hiapo, but should not be assumed to appear on every example or asserted as mandatory features of this one.

### Context

Nineteenth-century Niuean hiapo developed through local practice and regional encounters, including missionary networks. Evidence of an earlier local barkcloth tradition is possible; missionary arrival should not be described as the certain invention of Niuean barkcloth. Historic designs continue to inform contemporary artists.

### Recognition Anchors

- Dense concentric geometric organization.
- Vegetal imagery around outer zones.
- Fine painted lines on a pale barkcloth surface.

### Comparisons

AP #223, Presentation of Fijian mats and tapa cloths to Queen Elizabeth II (`ap223-fijian-presentation`): Both show the social value of fiber arts. Hiapo centers on a painted barkcloth object, while the Fijian presentation combines worn masi, carried woven mats and procession in an exchange event.

## AP #220: Tamati Waka Nene

### Identification

- Maker/culture: Gottfried Lindauer (1839–1926); Māori sitter; Gottfried Lindauer's New Zealand portrait practice.
- Date: 1890 C.E.
- Medium: Oil on canvas.
- Type and period: Posthumous painted ancestral portrait; Late 19th-century New Zealand portraiture.
- Source geography: New Zealand (Lindauer's portrait practice).
- Learning group: Monuments & Ancestors.

The portrait belongs to Lindauer's New Zealand practice. Nene's Hokianga affiliation is the sitter's context, not proof of the painting's studio; Auckland is the current collection, not an asserted 1890 workshop.

### Function

The portrait records a Māori rangatira and can also sustain ancestral presence as a treasured taonga. European collecting and Māori family engagements with portraits need not have identical purposes.

### Form

Oil-paint naturalism models the face and clothing through light and shadow. The sitter and upright tewhatewha dominate the foreground; intricate moko contrasts with the softer texture of the feather cloak.

### Content

Nene wears a kahu kiwi cloak and a pounamu earring and holds a tewhatewha weapon. Facial moko and valued possessions communicate identity, mana and standing, rather than functioning as generic exotic decoration.

### Context

Nene died in 1871, before Lindauer arrived in New Zealand; the 1890 painting is posthumous and likely drew on a John Crombie photograph. It is neither that photograph nor Charles F. Goldie's later portrait. Lindauer's rendering can include artistic interventions, not merely photographic transcription.

### Recognition Anchors

- Intricate facial moko.
- Kiwi-feather cloak and pounamu earring.
- Upright tewhatewha in the sitter's hand.

### Comparisons

AP #214, Moai on platform (ahu) (`ap214-moai-ahu`): Both can sustain an ancestor's presence among descendants. Lindauer works through oil-paint naturalism and photographic mediation, whereas moai use monumental stone, platforms and ritual engagement with the eyes.

### Required Views and Sources

## AP #221: Navigation chart

### Identification

- Maker/culture: Marshallese navigators and chart makers; individual maker unrecorded; Marshallese; Micronesian.
- Date: 19th to early 20th century C.E.
- Medium: Wood and fiber.
- Type and period: Stick navigation chart; Marshallese ocean-navigation knowledge.
- Source geography: Marshall Islands, Micronesia.
- Learning group: Navigation & Knowledge.

The Marshallese navigational tradition anchors this portable chart; a precise island, maker and workshop are not established by the CED.

### Function

Used on shore as a mnemonic and teaching aid for relationships among swells, currents and islands. Navigators internalized the knowledge; the chart was not a GPS-like device consulted continuously at sea.

### Form

Straight, diagonal and curved members are lashed into an open framework. The abstract lattice models relationships in moving water rather than simply tracing coastlines or preserving uniform geographic scale.

### Content

The chart concerns how islands and ocean swells interact and how navigators recognize routes. Shell island markers occur in related charts, but the CED specifies wood and fiber for this selected work; related examples should not overwrite its identification.

### Context

Marshallese navigation relies on apprenticeship and embodied sensitivity to wave motion. The ocean links communities rather than constituting empty space between them. Charts reflect learned and often maker-specific knowledge, not a universally standardized cartographic code.

### Recognition Anchors

- Open rectangular lattice.
- Crossing diagonals and curved members.
- Visible lashings at the joints.

### Comparisons

AP #213, Nan Madol (`ap213-nan-madol`): Both treat water as connection. The chart teaches ocean relationships through an abstract framework, while Nan Madol organizes built islets through navigable canals; one is a knowledge model, the other an architectural environment.

## AP #222: Malagan display and mask

### Identification

- Maker/culture: New Ireland carvers and ritual communities; individual makers unrecorded; New Ireland communities; Papua New Guinea.
- Date: 20th century C.E.
- Medium: Wood, pigment, fiber, and shell.
- Type and period: Mortuary display and ceremonial mask; New Ireland malagan mortuary and performance traditions.
- Source geography: New Ireland, Papua New Guinea.
- Learning group: Ritual & Performance.

New Ireland malagan traditions anchor the mask and display. Their current Pennsylvania and Basel museum settings do not establish an original carving workshop or a single shared ceremony.

### Function

Malagan ceremonies commemorate the dead, conclude mourning obligations and reorganize relations among the living. Revealed carvings, feasting and exchanges work together, while masks can participate in performance.

### Form

The first required view presents a crested mask with a painted face and varied textures. The second shows multiple elaborate openwork carvings beneath a thatched shelter. The mask and the displayed ensemble require separate formal descriptions.

### Content

Intertwined human and animal motifs can carry social memory and lineage associations. Their meanings and rights vary by community and occasion; no single fixed symbolic dictionary explains all malagan.

### Context

New Ireland contains diverse communities and traditions. The CED's mask and Basel museum display are not proof of one shared historical ceremony. Elaborate carvings may be discarded or transferred after ritual use; their temporary activation does not mean they were carelessly made.

### Recognition Anchors

- High-crested painted mask.
- Multiple elaborate carvings under a shelter in the second view.
- Openwork combinations of human and animal imagery.

### Comparisons

AP #218, Buk (mask) (`ap218-buk-mask`): Both depend on materials and performance, but Buk is recognized by its turtle-shell bird-human assemblage. Malagan requires attention to both the mask and mortuary display without equating distinct community practices.

### Required Views and Sources

1. `malagan-mask`: Crested mask at left in the CED; separately identified from the display. [College Board CED, physical p. 280 / printed p. 273](https://apcentral.collegeboard.org/media/pdf/ap-art-history-course-and-exam-description.pdf).

The documented performance occurred in Fiji. The CED and supplied Smarthistory chapter do not securely establish the precise venue or photograph date; the Wellington archive is not the performance site.

### Function

The presentation welcomed royal visitors through valued fiber goods, bodily performance and gathered witnesses. It activated traditions of reciprocity and tribute within a colonial encounter rather than serving as a display of anonymous craft products.

### Form

The photograph shows women moving in a line with rolled mats, patterned masi clothing and a seated audience. The performance also involved movement, chant, costume and sensory elements that a still photograph cannot preserve fully.

### Content

Carried woven mats and worn barkcloth are directly visible. The photograph does not visibly document every cloth being handed to the Queen. Pandanus mats are woven, while masi is made from beaten bark; the two techniques are distinct.

### Context

The CED identifies the 1953 royal visit to Fiji. The source explicitly states that the exact photograph date is unknown, and it does not securely establish the precise venue. The Wellington archive is a holding institution, not the place of performance.
