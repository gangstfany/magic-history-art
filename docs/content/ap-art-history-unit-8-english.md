## AP #192 — Great Stupa at Sanchi

### Identification

Buddhist patrons, builders and relief sculptors; individual makers unrecorded. Buddhist; Maurya and late Shunga periods. c. 300 B.C.E.–100 C.E.. Stone masonry; sandstone facing on dome. Buddhist reliquary monument. Original-context anchor: Sanchi, Madhya Pradesh, India. Historical period: Mauryan origins and later Shunga/Satavahana enlargement.

### Function

Enshrines relics and supports clockwise circumambulation as embodied Buddhist devotion; the solid mound is not a congregational interior.

### Form

A hemispherical anda, stone railings, four carved toranas and a crowning chatra organize concentric paths and a cosmic vertical axis.

### Content

Gateway reliefs use Jataka narratives and symbols such as trees, wheels and empty thrones. Later anthropomorphic Buddha images beside the paths belong to subsequent phases.

### Context

The monument began in Ashoka's Mauryan period and was enlarged and embellished under later patrons. The present ensemble should not be assigned wholesale to its earliest foundation.

### Recognition Anchors

- Solid hemispherical mound with crowning umbrellas
- Carved multi-beam gateways
- Railings and two circumambulatory levels

### Comparisons

AP #198 — Borobudur Temple: Both organize Buddhist circumambulation: Sanchi circles a mound, while Borobudur combines ascent through terraces with narrative reliefs.

## AP #193 — Terra cotta warriors from mausoleum of the first Qin emperor of China

### Identification

Qin imperial workshops; potters and sculptors; commissioned by Qin Shi Huang. Qin dynasty China. c. 221–209 B.C.E.. Painted terra cotta. Funerary sculptural army. Original-context anchor: Qin imperial mausoleum, Lintong, near Xi'an, China. Historical period: First unified Chinese empire.

### Function

A protective funerary army extends the emperor's command into the afterlife and demonstrates the state's ability to mobilize labor.

### Form

Near-life-size figures combine repeated poses and assembled components with individually finished faces, hair and clothing. Originally painted surfaces differed from today's exposed clay.

### Content

Infantry, archers, officers and horses create a military ensemble. Variation does not prove that each warrior is a portrait of a particular soldier.

### Context

Qin unification and standardization frame this enormous imperial project. The pits discovered in 1974 are parts of a much larger mausoleum complex, not the opened imperial burial chamber.

### Recognition Anchors

- Rows of life-size soldiers
- Detailed armor and differentiated hair and faces
- Horses within military formations

### Comparisons

AP #194 — Funeral banner of Lady Dai (Xin Zhui): Both preserve elite identity after death: the army uses military scale and collective order, whereas Lady Dai's banner presents an individual's place in a layered cosmos.

## AP #194 — Funeral banner of Lady Dai (Xin Zhui)

### Identification

Han silk painters; individual maker unrecorded. Western Han dynasty China. c. 180 B.C.E.. Painted silk. T-shaped funerary silk banner. Original-context anchor: Mawangdui Tomb 1, Changsha, China. Historical period: Early Western Han elite funerary culture.

The anchor marks the excavated funerary context, not a securely documented silk-painting workshop.

### Function

Associated with mourning and the afterlife; proposed uses include identifying the deceased or aiding the soul's passage. Its precise ritual function remains debated.

### Form

The T-shaped painted silk is organized in horizontal zones joined by sinuous dragons. Overlapping bodies and changing scale suggest spatial depth.

### Content

Celestial bodies and deities occupy the top; Lady Dai appears with a staff and attendants; mourning rites and subterranean beings appear below.

### Context

Excavated from Mawangdui Tomb 1, the banner lay above the innermost coffin. The CED's approximate 180 B.C.E. label is retained, while scholarship often uses a broader second-century date. The findspot does not establish a workshop.

### Recognition Anchors

- T-shaped silk with pendants
- An elderly woman with staff and attendants
- Sun and moon above; dragons and fish below

### Comparisons

AP #193 — Terra cotta warriors from mausoleum of the first Qin emperor of China: The banner links cosmic realms through painting; the Qin army sustains imperial military authority through massed three-dimensional figures.

## AP #195 — Longmen caves

### Identification

Successive Buddhist patrons and stone carvers; Gaozong and Wu Zetian associated with Fengxian. Chinese Buddhism; Northern Wei through Tang and later dynasties. 493–1127 C.E.. Limestone. Buddhist rock-cut cave and monumental sculptural complex. Original-context anchor: Longmen, Luoyang, China. Historical period: Northern Wei foundations; major Tang commissions; later additions.

### Function

Devotional images and donor commissions generate merit; monumental imperial patronage connects Buddhist hierarchy with political authority.

### Form

Caves and open ensembles are cut into riverside limestone. At Fengxian, the serene monumental Vairocana contrasts with muscular, animated guardians.

### Content

Required views distinguish the cliff setting, a guardian and Vairocana's attendants. Disciples, bodhisattvas and heavenly kings occupy different roles.

### Context

Work began under the Northern Wei in 493, with major Tang commissions such as Fengxian and subsequent additions. The CED's Tang label should not erase the site's earlier history.

### Recognition Anchors

- Riverside cliff filled with niches
- Colossal seated Buddha and ranked attendants
- Dynamic muscular guardians

### Comparisons

AP #197 — Todai-ji: Both use Vairocana and state patronage; Longmen fixes the sacred ensemble in a cliff, while Todai-ji houses a bronze Buddha in monumental timber architecture.

## AP #196 — Gold and jade crown

### Identification

Silla goldworkers; individual maker unrecorded. Silla kingdom, Korea; Three Kingdoms period. Fifth to sixth century C.E.. Metalwork; gold and jade. Royal ceremonial and funerary crown. Original-context anchor: North mound of Hwangnam Daechong Tomb, Gyeongju, Korea. Historical period: Fifth–sixth-century Silla elite ritual and burial.

Smarthistory identifies the excavated burial context; this does not establish the precise goldworking workshop.

### Function

Royal ceremonial regalia signaled rank and could accompany the owner into burial. Fragility alone does not establish an exclusively funerary use.

### Form

Thin gold elements rise as branching trees and antler-like forms; dangling discs, chains and comma-shaped jade gogok create movement and reflected light.

### Content

Natural forms are associated with sacred-tree imagery, fertility and shamanic traditions; these readings are interpretive rather than fixed names for every projection.

### Context

This specific crown was excavated from the north mound of Hwangnam Daechong in Gyeongju. Silla metalwork reflects elite ritual and interregional exchange; the findspot is not a known workshop.

### Recognition Anchors

- Branching and antler-like uprights
- Green comma-shaped jade pendants
- Thin gold band with dangling ornaments

### Comparisons

AP #205 — Portrait of Sin Sukju (1417–1475): Both communicate Korean elite rank through dress: Silla regalia uses precious materials and nature imagery, while the Joseon portrait uses a rank badge and restrained official decorum.

## AP #197 — Todai-ji

### Identification

Various artists; Unkei, Kaikei and the Kei School associated with Nio guardians. Japanese Buddhism; Nara foundation and later rebuilding. 743 C.E.; rebuilt c. 1700 C.E.. Bronze and wood sculpture; wood architecture with ceramic-tile roofs. Buddhist temple, monumental Buddha and guardian ensemble. Original-context anchor: Todai-ji, Nara, Japan. Historical period: Nara imperial Buddhism; Kamakura sculpture; Edo rebuilding.

### Function

Emperor Shomu's project served as the center of a state-sponsored temple network, joining Buddhist protection to spiritual and political unity.

### Form

The timber Great Buddha Hall houses a colossal bronze seated Buddha. The Great South Gate uses massive timber construction and dynamic joined-wood Nio guardians.

### Content

The Great Buddha is Vairocana. Agyo's open mouth and Ungyo's closed mouth distinguish the paired gate guardians; each component has a separate identity.

### Context

The 743 date marks the initial commission; the consecration was in 752. Fires led to repeated restoration. The present hall was completed in 1709, while the Nio guardians date to 1203, not the Nara foundation.

### Recognition Anchors

- Monumental tiled timber hall
- Colossal bronze Buddha on lotus pedestal
- Open- and closed-mouth muscular guardians

### Comparisons

AP #195 — Longmen caves: Both combine a monumental Buddha with guardians and state patronage; Todai-ji's bronze and timber ensemble differs materially from Longmen's rock-cut sculpture.

## AP #198 — Borobudur Temple

### Identification

Sailendra patrons; architects and stone carvers unrecorded. Mahayana Buddhism; Sailendra dynasty, Central Java. c. 750–842 C.E.. Volcanic-stone masonry. Terraced Buddhist pilgrimage monument. Original-context anchor: Borobudur, Central Java, Indonesia. Historical period: Eighth–ninth-century Javanese Buddhist kingship.

### Function

Circumambulation and ascent guide a Buddhist pilgrimage, bringing narrative teachings into an embodied sequence of movement.

### Form

Square lower terraces give way to circular upper platforms, forming a three-dimensional mandala crowned by a central stupa and perforated bell-shaped stupas.

### Content

Buddhist narrative reliefs line the lower routes; numerous Buddhas and the upper stupas structure the sacred program. Empty niches today do not necessarily reflect the original installation.

### Context

Sailendra patronage in Java participated in transregional Buddhist networks. Abandonment and modern restoration belong to its later history and must be distinguished from the original construction.

### Recognition Anchors

- Square terraces beneath circular platforms
- Perforated bell-shaped stupas
- Narrative reliefs lining the paths

### Comparisons

AP #192 — Great Stupa at Sanchi: Sanchi centers devotion on a mound and gateways; Borobudur integrates circumambulation, narrative and vertical progression.

## AP #199 — Angkor, the temple of Angkor Wat, and the city of Angkor Thom, Cambodia

### Identification

Suryavarman II associated with Angkor Wat; Jayavarman VII with Angkor Thom and Bayon; builders unrecorded. Khmer Angkor kingdom; Hindu and Buddhist royal foundations. c. 800–1400 C.E.. Stone masonry; sandstone. Royal temple and urban sacred complex. Original-context anchor: Angkor, near Siem Reap, Cambodia. Historical period: Angkorian urban and temple development.

### Function

Angkor Wat was founded for Vishnu and royal authority. Jayavarman VII's Angkor Thom and Bayon belong to a distinct Buddhist royal building program.

### Form

Angkor Wat's five towers, axial galleries and moat visualize Mount Meru and its surrounding cosmos. Bayon's face towers and Angkor Thom's South Gate are separate components.

### Content

The Churning of the Ocean of Milk relief shows gods and demons pulling a naga to obtain amrita. Bayon's faces have Buddhist, bodhisattva and royal interpretations; the CED's caption is not a resolution of that debate.

### Context

Angkor Wat largely dates to the early twelfth century; Angkor Thom and Bayon began under Jayavarman VII later that century. The broad 800–1400 range describes development. The temple plan and regional site plan are distinct required views.

### Recognition Anchors

- Five lotus-bud towers and surrounding moat
- Churning relief with a long serpent
- Bayon face towers and monumental city gate

### Comparisons

AP #200 — Lakshmana Temple: Both use royal patronage and mountain-like Hindu sanctuaries; Angkor Wat's vast galleries and moat contrast with Lakshmana's compact platform and shikhara. Bayon must retain its separate Buddhist context.

## AP #200 — Lakshmana Temple

### Identification

Chandella royal patrons and sandstone builders; individual makers unrecorded. Hindu; Chandella dynasty, north India. c. 930–950 C.E.. Sandstone. Nagara-style Hindu temple. Original-context anchor: Khajuraho, Madhya Pradesh, India. Historical period: Tenth-century Chandella temple patronage.

### Function

Dedicated to Vishnu, the temple guides worshippers through mandapas toward the garbhagriha and provides for circumambulation.

### Form

A raised platform supports a principal shrine and four subsidiary shrines in a panchayatana layout. A clustered curving shikhara produces a mountain-like silhouette.

### Content

Deities, embracing mithuna couples and worldly scenes coexist on the exterior. Erotic imagery is only part of the program; the required lion-like creature and figure detail also matters.

### Context

Chandella patronage at Khajuraho joined religious devotion with royal prestige. The temple's name should not replace identification of its Vaikuntha Vishnu dedication.

### Recognition Anchors

- Elevated platform and clustered curving tower
- Dense figural bands including mithuna
- Axial porch, halls and inner sanctuary

### Comparisons

AP #199 — Angkor, the temple of Angkor Wat, and the city of Angkor Thom, Cambodia: Lakshmana uses a north Indian Nagara tower and platform; Angkor Wat presents the sacred mountain through five towers, galleries and a moat.

## AP #201 — Travelers among Mountains and Streams

### Identification

Fan Kuan. Chinese; Northern Song landscape painting. c. 1000 C.E.. Ink and colors on silk. Landscape hanging scroll. Original-context anchor: Northern Song China (workshop uncertain). Historical period: Early Northern Song monumental landscape.

The landscape evokes northern Chinese mountains; neither its depicted setting nor its present collection proves an exact production site.

### Function

Invites close viewing and contemplation of humanity's place in nature rather than documenting an exact itinerary.

### Form

A towering peak dominates the vertical silk. Foreground rocks, middle-ground trees, ink textures and mist-like blank passages establish changing spatial depths.

### Content

A tiny pack-animal caravan contrasts with the massive mountain; a slender waterfall links the heights with the lower landscape.

### Context

Northern Song monumental landscape combines observation with ideals of natural order. Daoist resonances are useful interpretations, but the depicted terrain and present collection do not establish an exact studio.

### Recognition Anchors

- Huge central mountain
- Slender waterfall and band of mist
- Tiny caravan near the base

### Comparisons

AP #211 — Under the Wave off Kanagawa (Kanagawa oki nami ura), also known as the Great Wave, from the series Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji: Both contrast small people with powerful nature; Fan Kuan uses textured ink and vertical mountain mass, while Hokusai uses a colored print and near-wave/far-mountain contrast.

## AP #202 — Shiva as Lord of Dance (Nataraja)

### Identification

Chola bronze casters; individual maker unrecorded. Hindu; Chola dynasty, Tamil Nadu. c. eleventh century C.E.. Cast bronze. Hindu processional devotional sculpture. Original-context anchor: Tamil Nadu, India (Chola production context). Historical period: Chola bronze devotional tradition.

The precise casting workshop and original temple are not established by the CED; this is a regional creation-context anchor.

### Function

As a devotional image suitable for dressing and procession, Shiva becomes accessible to worshippers beyond a fixed sanctuary.

### Form

Lost-wax casting creates a balanced four-armed dancer within a flaming aureole, with one foot on a dwarf and the other raised.

### Content

The drum signifies creation, fire destruction, abhaya reassurance and the raised foot liberation. The dwarf Apasmara embodies ignorance.

### Context

Chola patrons supported temple bronzes in Tamil Nadu. Cosmic cycles operate alongside lived devotion; neither an exact casting workshop nor the original temple is established here.

### Recognition Anchors

- Four-armed dancer inside flame ring
- Dwarf beneath one foot and raised other foot
- Drum, flame and flowing locks

### Comparisons

AP #192 — Great Stupa at Sanchi: Nataraja joins devotion to a movable god-image and cosmic dance; Sanchi organizes the worshipper's movement around a fixed reliquary.

The narrative depicts the Sanjo Palace attack in Kyoto in 1159; the depicted event is not proof of the later handscroll's production workshop.

### Function

An elite audience unrolled a war-tale narrative that shaped memory of conflict between court factions and warrior clans.

### Form

The emaki unfolds from right to left. Riders, diagonal rooflines, flames and dense crowds control tempo as successive portions are exposed.

### Content

The narrative recounts the 1159 attack on Sanjo Palace, fire, violence and the abduction of retired emperor Go-Shirakawa. Opening calligraphy and riders are required alongside the full scroll.

### Context

Painted roughly a century after the event, the scroll reflects Kamakura-period retrospective storytelling. Kyoto's narrative setting does not prove the later painting workshop's location.

### Comparisons

## AP #204 — The David Vases

### Identification

The anchor represents porcelain production at Jingdezhen, not the later temple dedication or present London collection.

### Function

Made as temple altar offerings, originally associated with an incense burner, the vases express a donor's wishes for family well-being.

### Form

Cobalt designs are painted beneath a transparent glaze on high-fired white porcelain. Tall necks, elephant handles and banded decoration produce a matching pair.

### Content

Dragons, phoenixes, floral bands and dated donor inscriptions appear together. The inscriptions establish 1351; missing handle rings are a condition issue.

Jingdezhen production and cobalt exchange reveal interregional connections under the Yuan, not a Ming origin. Production site, temple destination and later London collection are different places.

- Elephant handles and dragon bands
- Long inscriptions on the necks

### Comparisons

## AP #206 — Forbidden City

### Identification

Imperial architects and craftspeople; Ming Yongle and later Qing patrons. Chinese; Ming and Qing imperial courts. Fifteenth century C.E. and later. Stone masonry, marble, brick, wood and ceramic tile. Imperial palace and ceremonial residential complex. Original-context anchor: Forbidden City, Beijing, China. Historical period: Fifteenth-century Ming palace with Qing additions.

### Function

Imperial ceremony, governance and residence are distributed by rank. Outer and Inner Courts translate political and familial hierarchy into space.

### Form

A north-south axis passes through gates, courts and elevated halls. Timber buildings, red walls, yellow glazed roofs and dragon imagery reinforce imperial status.

### Content

Required components include the overview, Tiananmen, Hall of Supreme Harmony, plan and the illusionistically painted Tranquility and Longevity interior. Its painted architecture, garden and ceiling cannot be replaced by a generic hall.

### Context

The main Ming palace was built in 1406–1420; Qing patrons maintained and expanded it. Qianlong's retirement interiors belong to the eighteenth century, not the original Ming campaign. The CED front-gate photograph shows Tiananmen rather than Wumen.

### Recognition Anchors

- Axial sequence of gates, courts and halls
- Red walls, yellow roofs and marble platforms
- Illusionistic murals and painted ceiling in the required interior

### Comparisons

AP #209 — Taj Mahal: Both use axial symmetry and controlled access for imperial authority; the Forbidden City serves living governance and residence, while the Taj is a commemorative mausoleum complex.

## AP #207 — Ryoan-ji

### Identification

Garden designer unrecorded; attribution uncertain. Japanese; Zen Buddhism, Muromachi foundation and later garden history. c. 1480 C.E.; current design most likely eighteenth century C.E.. Rock garden; rocks, gravel, moss, walls; pond garden. Zen temple garden complex. Original-context anchor: Ryoan-ji, Kyoto, Japan. Historical period: Muromachi temple; likely eighteenth-century current garden design.

### Function

Provides a setting for sustained looking and contemplation in a Zen temple; a single fixed doctrinal meaning for each rock is not established.

### Form

The dry garden places fifteen rocks on moss patches within raked gravel and low walls. The separate wet garden includes a real pond; the plan situates both.

### Content

The dry arrangement can suggest mountains, water or islands without literal flowing water. Shifting viewpoints change how the stones relate to one another.

### Context

The temple's early history is distinct from the present garden design. The CED retains approximately 1480 but states that the current design most likely dates to the eighteenth century; authorship is uncertain.

### Recognition Anchors

- Fifteen rocks and raked pale gravel
- Moss islands and broad empty intervals
- Separate pond garden and bridge

### Comparisons

AP #198 — Borobudur Temple: Ryoan-ji emphasizes abstract forms and sustained contemplation; Borobudur guides movement through ascent, circumambulation and narrative relief.

### Identification

Bichitr's court context is established, but the CED does not fix the city where this portable album painting was made.

### Function

A luxury album image for courtly viewing asserts the ruler's preference for spiritual wisdom while placing him above other worldly sovereigns.

### Form

Hierarchical scale enlarges Jahangir; the sun-and-moon halo and hourglass throne connect him with cosmic and temporal power. Fine brushwork and the decorated border produce a complete album page.

### Content

The emperor gives a book to a Sufi shaikh. Below stand an Ottoman ruler, James I and Bichitr's self-portrait; putti and European-derived imagery contribute to the allegory.

- Ranked visitors and ornamental border

## AP #209 — Taj Mahal

### Identification

Ustad Ahmad Lahori supervising masons, marble workers, mosaicists and decorators. Mughal India; Islamic funerary and garden architecture. 1632–1653 C.E.. Stone masonry and marble with precious and semiprecious stone inlay; gardens. Imperial mausoleum and garden complex. Original-context anchor: Agra, Uttar Pradesh, India. Historical period: Seventeenth-century Shah Jahan imperial patronage.

### Function

Shah Jahan commissioned the mausoleum for Mumtaz Mahal, combining personal commemoration, dynastic prestige and paradisiacal garden imagery.

### Form

A white marble dome, four corner minarets, axial reflecting pool and subdivided charbagh create a controlled symmetrical ensemble.

### Content

Interior cenotaphs are symbolic markers; the actual burials lie below. A carved marble screen, floral stone inlay and calligraphy reward close viewing.

### Context

The 1632–1653 range covers development of the wider complex. Shah Jahan was later buried beside Mumtaz Mahal. Mughal design depends on multiple craft traditions and subsequent conservation should not be confused with initial construction.

- White bulbous dome and four minarets
- Axial reflecting pool and garden
- Cenotaphs within carved marble screen

### Comparisons

## AP #210 — White and Red Plum Blossoms

### Identification

Ogata Korin. Japanese; Edo-period Rinpa painting. c. 1710–1716 C.E.. Ink, watercolor and gold leaf on paper. Pair of two-fold painted screens. Original-context anchor: Edo-period Japan (workshop uncertain). Historical period: Early eighteenth-century decorative painting.

Ogata Korin worked in Kyoto and Edo; the CED does not establish a precise studio for these screens. Atami is the present collection, not the creation site.

### Function

Folding screens divide and embellish interior space; precious gold and seasonal imagery serve decorative display and connoisseurship.

### Form

The paired two-fold screens connect through a curving stream. Flat gold contrasts with tree forms, while wet-on-wet tarashikomi creates mottled bark and stylized water patterns.

### Content

The left screen bears white plum blossoms and the right red blossoms. Both halves are required to understand the balance of trees and stream.

### Context

Rinpa is a later label for a broad design lineage rather than an academy founded by Korin. His Kyoto and Edo activities do not establish a precise studio for these screens; Atami is their present collection.

### Recognition Anchors

- Paired two-fold screens on gold
- White blossoms left; red blossoms right
- Curving patterned stream and mottled trunks

## AP #211 — Under the Wave off Kanagawa (Kanagawa oki nami ura), also known as the Great Wave, from the series Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji

### Identification

Katsushika Hokusai; collaborative woodblock production. Japanese; Edo-period ukiyo-e print culture. 1830–1833 C.E.. Polychrome woodblock print; ink and color on paper. Commercial landscape print from a series. Original-context anchor: Edo (Tokyo), Japan. Historical period: Nineteenth-century Edo commercial landscape prints.

The anchor represents the print's Edo publishing context, not the depicted waters off Kanagawa.

### Function

A reproducible commercial ukiyo-e print offered images of Fuji and travel scenery to an urban audience, rather than functioning as a unique oil painting.

### Form

The enormous foreground wave curls over a tiny distant Fuji. Claw-like foam, diagonal boats, flat color and Prussian blue create dramatic near-far contrasts.

### Content

Three long boats with crouching crews face the wave; distant Fuji supplies a stable counterpoint. The image need not be identified as a documented tsunami.

### Context

Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji responded to travel and sacred-mountain interests. European-derived perspective and imported blue intersect with Edo publishing; the depicted Kanagawa waters are not the production site.

### Recognition Anchors

- Claw-like white foam on curling wave
- Three slender boats and crouching crews
- Small triangular Fuji in the distance

### Comparisons

An enlarged solitary figure, low horizon, illuminated face and windblown robe make forward movement monumental; misty landscape accompanies idealized socialist-realist figuration.

### Content

A young Mao carries an umbrella on his way to Anyuan. The image's narrative refers to a 1921 visit; the miners' strike of 1922 is related background, not the same moment.

The CED specifies an anonymous circa-1969 lithograph after Liu Chunhua's 1967 painting. Cultural Revolution propaganda recast revolutionary leadership; neither the artist's location nor Anyuan identifies the anonymous printer's workshop.
