## AP #167 · Conical tower and circular wall of Great Zimbabwe

### Identification

Shona peoples, southeastern Zimbabwe; c. 1000–1400 C.E.; makers unrecorded. Coursed granite blocks, assembled without mortar. Monumental architecture within an African urban and royal center.

### Function

The Great Enclosure expressed elite authority and organized access to royal space. The conical tower's precise purpose is uncertain; a symbolic granary connecting rulership with agricultural abundance is one interpretation, not a proven storage function.

### Content

A solid tapered tower stands inside curving enclosure walls. Hill, enclosure, and valley complexes belong to the larger settlement.

### Form

Dry-stone construction uses carefully laid horizontal courses. Massive curving walls and narrow passages shape movement; chevron patterns articulate masonry.

### Context

Local African builders developed this architecture within a wealthy center linked to long-distance trade. Colonial claims of foreign construction denied African achievement and are discredited.

### Recognition Anchors

Granite dry-stone walls; rounded enclosure; solid conical tower. Not an adobe mosque or a European fortress.

### Comparisons

AP #168, Great Mosque of Djenné: both monumental African buildings express communal or political identity through local materials. Zimbabwe uses enduring stone; Djenné's adobe depends on repeated communal maintenance.

## AP #168 · Great Mosque of Djenné

### Identification

Djenné, Mali; founded c. 1200 C.E.; rebuilt 1906–1907. Adobe architecture by local masons; the rebuilding is associated with master mason Ismaila Traoré. Congregational mosque in a historic Islamic trading town.

### Function

Provides space for Muslim communal prayer and sustains civic identity. Annual replastering joins practical conservation with community participation.

### Content

The prayer hall and courtyard relate to the qibla wall facing Mecca. Three prominent towers punctuate the façade; the adjacent square hosts the Monday market.

### Form

Mud-brick walls have an earthen plaster surface. Buttresses and projecting timber torons create rhythmic relief; the torons also provide access for replastering.

### Context

The mosque combines Islamic worship with regional earthen-building expertise. The present structure belongs to a colonial-era rebuilding, not an untouched thirteenth-century building. Seasonal weather makes renewal essential.

### Recognition Anchors

Earth-colored façade; three tower forms; projecting wooden torons; market square. Link material to maintenance, not merely appearance.

### Comparisons

AP #56, Great Mosque of Córdoba: both organize congregational worship around a qibla and prayer hall. Córdoba's stone-and-brick arcades contrast with Djenné's adobe walls and collective replastering.

## AP #169 · Wall plaque, from Oba's palace

### Identification

Edo peoples, Kingdom of Benin, present-day Nigeria; 16th century C.E. Cast-brass relief by royal brass-casting guild artists, individual maker unrecorded. Palace art commissioned within the Oba's court.

### Function

Decorated palace courtyard posts and communicated the Oba's authority, court hierarchy, and royal history to viewers.

### Content

An equestrian Oba occupies the center, surrounded by attendants of different sizes. Regalia and the horse distinguish royal status.

### Form

Lost-wax casting produces high-relief figures against an ornamented rectangular ground. Hierarchical scale makes the ruler largest: this is a meaningful convention, not an inability to depict depth.

### Context

Trade with Portuguese merchants helped supply metals and luxury goods supporting court production. The British invasion of Benin in 1897 dispersed palace objects; current museum display is distinct from their original royal setting.

### Recognition Anchors

Brass rectangle; dominant mounted king; smaller attendants; dense patterned background. Benin is the historical kingdom in Nigeria, not the modern Republic of Benin.

### Comparisons

AP #180, Olowe's veranda post: both communicate royal power through palace decoration and scale. The plaque emphasizes the king's dominance; Olowe's group makes his dependence on his senior wife visible.

## AP #170 · Sika dwa kofi (Golden Stool)

### Identification

Asante (Ashanti) peoples, south-central Ghana; c. 1700 C.E.; maker unrecorded. Gold over wood with cast-gold attachments. Sacred royal stool associated with the unification of the Asante state.

### Function

Embodies the collective soul and continuity of the Asante people. It legitimizes kingship but is not an ordinary seat for the king.

### Content

A curved seat rises above a supporting central structure and base; gold fittings and bells mark exceptional status.

### Form

Gold transforms a familiar stool form into sacred regalia. Its curved seat, supports, and gleaming surface carry meaning beyond useful furniture.

### Context

Asante tradition recounts that priest Okomfo Anokye brought the stool from heaven to Osei Tutu. Treat this as the community's sacred origin account. A British demand to possess the stool helped provoke resistance in 1900. It remained a protected symbol of sovereignty.

### Recognition Anchors

Gold-covered curved stool; ceremonial protection; never confuse symbolic enthronement with physically sitting on this sacred object.

### Comparisons

AP #171, Ndop of King Mishe: both embody authority beyond a ruler's physical presence. The ndop identifies one historical king; the Golden Stool represents the whole Asante nation.

## AP #171 · Ndop (portrait figure) of King Mishe miShyaang maMbul

### Identification

Kuba peoples, present-day Democratic Republic of the Congo; c. 1760–1780 C.E.; court carver unrecorded. Wooden commemorative royal sculpture representing King Mishe miShyaang maMbul.

### Function

Preserves the king's reign and provides a focus for royal memory and spiritual presence. It is an idealized royal portrait, not a naturalistic likeness.

### Content

The king sits cross-legged on a decorated platform, wearing royal regalia and holding a ceremonial knife. His identifying ibol is a drum with a hand motif before the figure.

### Form

An enlarged head, composed expression, rounded modeling, and balanced seated pose express ideal kingship. Specific emblems distinguish rulers whose bodily conventions are otherwise similar.

### Context

Kuba court artists worked within specialized traditions. The drum connects this individual ruler's memory with royal performance and insignia; do not identify every ndop by the same emblem.

### Recognition Anchors

Cross-legged king; projecting headdress; decorated base; drum emblem. The contextual photograph shows another Kuba ruler, not Mishe himself.

### Comparisons

AP #177, Lukasa: both support political memory. Ndop materializes an individual king through regalia and an emblem; lukasa prompts specialists to perform broader histories.

## AP #172 · Power figure (Nkisi n'kondi)

### Identification

Kongo peoples, Central Africa; c. late 19th century C.E.; carver and activating ritual specialist unrecorded. Wood and metal; spiritually empowered sculpture. The exact workshop is unknown.

### Function

Helps enforce agreements, settle disputes, protect communities, and address wrongdoing. Its force depends on ritual activation, not carving alone.

### Content

A standing human figure bears many inserted nails or blades. A bodily cavity contains bilongo, potent substances prepared by a nganga, often sealed beneath a reflective surface.

### Form

An assertive stance and accumulated metal insertions make spiritual authority visible. Surface additions register successive interventions; the object develops through use.

### Context

A sculptor and nganga contribute different expertise. Kongo understandings connect material substances and spiritual action. Missionary confiscation and destruction disrupted these practices; calling the figure a “voodoo doll” misrepresents its juridical and protective role.

### Recognition Anchors

Wooden body studded with metal; forceful stance; medicine compartment. Link the nails to activation and obligations, not random aggression.

### Comparisons

AP #176, Ikenga: both are activated wooden shrine objects. Nkisi nkondi often addresses communal disputes and protection; ikenga emphasizes an owner's achievement and agency.

## AP #173 · Female (Pwo) mask

### Identification

Chokwe peoples, present-day Democratic Republic of the Congo and Angola cultural region; late 19th to early 20th century C.E.; maker unrecorded. Wood, fiber, pigment, and metal. Face mask created for masquerade; exact workshop unknown.

### Function

Honors ideal womanhood and female ancestral presence through performance, including settings associated with male initiation.

### Content

A female face bears carefully arranged scarification, coiffure, and refined features. Pwo imagery connects beauty with fertility and respected female roles.

### Form

A carved oval face, narrow eyes, modeled mouth, symmetrical markings, and attached fibers combine sculpture with costume. The static museum object omits movement and sound.

### Context

Men carve and perform Pwo masks; women are important participants and evaluators in the audience. A male performer embodying a female ideal differs from a woman wearing the mask. Interpretations and local practices vary.

### Recognition Anchors

Chokwe female face; patterned scarification; elaborate fiber hair; refined features. Identify culture before making a generic “African mask” claim.

### Comparisons

AP #175, Bundu mask: both express valued womanhood through masquerade. Pwo is danced by men; Sande's helmet-mask tradition is performed by women.

## AP #174 · Portrait mask (Mblo)

### Identification

Baule peoples, Côte d'Ivoire; early 20th century C.E.; Owie Kimou, carver. Wood and pigment, with brass details documented by Smarthistory. Portrait mask honoring Moya Yanso, commissioned within the Gbagba entertainment tradition of Kami.

### Function

Honors a respected woman and creates social enjoyment through masquerade. Its portrait identity involves a known person but idealizes moral and physical beauty.

### Content

Moya Yanso's face is represented with a high forehead, slender nose, restrained mouth, downcast eyes, and projections above the coiffure.

### Form

Polished wood, refined carving, and reflective brass heighten visual appeal in performance. Facial conventions express wisdom and modesty instead of photographic likeness.

### Context

Male dancers wore the mask with cloth costume and musical accompaniment. Moya Yanso could accompany her representation. The required contextual image shows her and her stepson holding the mask; it is not itself a photograph of active dancing.

### Recognition Anchors

Oval face; high forehead; narrow lowered eyes; six tubular crown projections. Remember both names: Moya Yanso and Owie Kimou.

### Comparisons

AP #171, Ndop: both are idealized representations of identifiable people. Mblo honors a community member through dance; ndop commemorates a ruler through an emblem.

## AP #175 · Bundu mask

### Identification

Sande Society, Mende peoples, forests of Sierra Leone and Liberia; 19th to 20th century C.E.; maker unrecorded. Wood, cloth, and fiber. Helmet mask associated with women's initiation and performed as part of a full costume.

### Function

Embodies Sande's guardian spirit and helps educate and incorporate girls into adult social roles.

### Content

An idealized female face has an elaborate coiffure, small features, and a ringed neck. The smooth black surface evokes valued beauty and the spirit's watery realm.

### Form

The hollow helmet encloses the head rather than simply covering the face. Restrained eyes and mouth, balanced carving, and polished finish express composure; fiber costume conceals the performer.

### Context

Initiated women perform this masquerade. It is a distinctive female-controlled masking tradition; avoid generalizing male masking practices to all African cultures. Neck rings have layered interpretations, including beauty and ripples associated with water.

### Recognition Anchors

Glossy black helmet; elaborate hair; small face; conspicuous neck rings; full fiber costume in context.

### Comparisons

AP #173, Pwo mask: both embody ideals of womanhood, but the Sande performer is female and the mask is helmet-shaped; the Chokwe Pwo is a face mask performed by men.

## AP #176 · Ikenga (shrine figure)

### Identification

Igbo peoples, southeastern Nigeria; c. 19th to 20th century C.E.; maker unrecorded. Carved wood. Personal shrine figure commissioned and consecrated for an owner; exact workshop unknown.

### Function

Supports and commemorates individual achievement, agency, and strength. Its efficacy is tied to the owner's actions and ritual relationship with the figure.

### Content

A horned human figure may carry a weapon in the right hand and a head or another status attribute in the left. Ram horns express determination.

### Form

Large horns dominate the silhouette. Seated posture and incised details may signify rank. Ikenga range from elaborate human figures to abstract forms, so not every example has identical attributes.

### Context

The “power of the right hand” links spiritual support with personal effort. Achievements may concern farming, warfare, trade, or other occupations. It is not a literal likeness of its owner.

### Recognition Anchors

Ram horns; right-hand emphasis; carved shrine figure. Explain the relationship between symbolic strength and achieved status.

### Comparisons

AP #170, Golden Stool: both connect objects with identity and authority. Ikenga usually concerns individual achievement; the Golden Stool embodies collective Asante identity and kingship.

## AP #177 · Lukasa (memory board)

### Identification

Mbudye Society, Luba peoples, present-day Democratic Republic of the Congo; c. 19th to 20th century C.E.; maker unrecorded. Wood, beads, and metal. Handheld mnemonic object used by trained historical specialists.

### Function

Prompts performed histories, genealogies, and knowledge supporting Luba political and spiritual institutions. It aids memory; it is not a text anyone can decode unaided.

### Content

Clusters of beads, pins, and other features evoke people, places, relationships, and events through learned associations.

### Form

A small shaped board supports tactile and visual configurations. Its narrowed center helps handling; placement, grouping, and touch matter as much as color.

### Context

Mbudye specialists recount knowledge through speech, song, and performance. Interpretation responds to audience and occasion, so the board is not a fixed chronological transcript or a universally readable map.

### Recognition Anchors

Palm-sized wooden board; beads and metal pins in clusters; tactile reading by a specialist.

### Comparisons

AP #171, Ndop: both sustain political memory. Ndop identifies a king through an emblem; lukasa activates a knowledgeable person's narration of relationships and history.

## AP #178 · Aka elephant mask

### Identification

Bamileke peoples, western Grassfields, Cameroon; c. 19th to 20th century C.E.; maker unrecorded. Wood, woven raffia, cloth, and beads. Masquerade ensemble associated with the elite Kuosi society; exact workshop unknown.

### Function

Expresses royal authority, elite status, and social order through public performance.

### Content

Broad ear discs and long panels evoke an elephant, an animal associated with the fon's power. Beaded geometry accompanies prestigious costume elements such as feathered headgear and leopard skin.

### Form

This is a flexible beaded textile mask, not simply a carved wooden elephant head. Repeated triangles and contrasting colors animate the surface as the dancer moves.

### Context

Kuosi members include titled and politically influential people. Their performance involves costume, music, and attendants; access to royal animal imagery signals authorized status. Imported beads demonstrate wealth and wider exchange.

### Recognition Anchors

Circular ear discs; long front trunk-like panel and rear panel; dense geometric beadwork; full masquerade costume.

### Comparisons

AP #175, Bundu mask: both become complete through performance. Aka announces elite court authority through animal references and beadwork; Bundu embodies Sande ideals through a polished wooden female helmet.

## AP #179 · Reliquary figure (byeri)

### Identification

Fang peoples, southern Cameroon cultural region; c. 19th to 20th century C.E. in the AP identity. Wood; reliquary guardian sculpture. The Brooklyn example is now attributed to an Ntem River Valley master and dated mid-18th to mid-19th century: distinguish the museum's revised cataloguing from AP metadata.

### Function

Guards ancestral relics in a bark container and mediates lineage memory and spiritual authority. Figures also participated in initiation instruction.

### Content

A human guardian combines a large head, youthful proportions, strong adult musculature, and prominent navel. These features connect life cycles and ancestral continuity.

### Form

Rounded limbs and elongated torso contrast with the restrained face. The figure's calm pose and muscular energy communicate controlled power, not naturalistic portraiture.

### Context

Relic containers preserved revered ancestors' remains within family custodianship. A detached museum figure hides the original figure-container relationship and its restricted ritual audience.

### Recognition Anchors

Large rounded head; tubular muscular limbs; prominent navel; composed expression. Name the missing bark reliquary context.

### Comparisons

AP #58, Church of Sainte-Foy's reliquary: both connect sculpted human imagery with powerful bodily relics. Sainte-Foy belongs to Christian pilgrimage; byeri concerns Fang lineage ancestors and particular custodians.

## AP #180 · Veranda post of enthroned king and senior wife (Opo Ogoga)

### Identification

Olowe of Ise, Yoruba peoples, Nigeria; c. 1910–1914 C.E. Wood and pigment. Carved architectural palace post commissioned for the Ogoga of Ikere; Ise identifies the artist's home and working center, not the palace's location.

### Function

Supports and embellishes a royal veranda while making the relationships sustaining kingship visible.

### Content

An enthroned king is accompanied by a much taller senior wife behind him, her hands on his throne. Smaller figures include a kneeling junior wife and flute-playing figure; a fan bearer is missing. The Art Institute identifies the flute player as Esu; Smarthistory describes a court messenger.

### Form

Elongated figures, forward-projecting heads, varied scale, and open spaces create an interdependent three-dimensional group. Surviving pigment recalls a once more colorful outdoor display.

### Context

The senior wife assisted coronation and used political and spiritual knowledge to support the king. Her size signals this importance without making her the king. Olowe's named authorship counters the stereotype of anonymous African art.

### Recognition Anchors

Seated crowned king; tall standing wife; smaller attendants; deep open carving. Do not substitute Olowe's mounted-warrior post for this work.

### Comparisons

AP #169, Benin palace plaque: both present court hierarchy. Olowe complicates simple rank-by-size: the senior wife's scale emphasizes the support behind the king's authority.
